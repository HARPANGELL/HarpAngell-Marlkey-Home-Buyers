How code enforcement works in Texas

A notice taped to the front door. A letter about a hearing on a "substandard structure." Fines that keep growing while you try to figure out how to pay for repairs you can't afford. If that's where you are, you're dealing with one of the most stressful parts of owning an older or damaged house, and you have more ways out than it feels like.

Texas cities enforce their property standards under state law, mainly Local Government Code Chapters 54 and 214 and Health and Safety Code Chapter 342. The details vary by city, but the path usually looks like this: someone reports a problem (often through 311), an inspector visits, you get a notice of violation with a deadline, and if it isn't resolved, the case goes to municipal court, an administrative hearing, or a building and standards commission.

Those numbers are why time matters here. A daily penalty that runs for months, plus a demolition lien, can eat much of the equity in a house.

Can a code lien lead to foreclosure in Texas?

For property that isn't your homestead, it can. A city can foreclose a Chapter 214 lien inside a delinquent property tax suit, or in court if the building was demolished, the lien has gone unpaid more than 180 days, and property taxes are delinquent (§ 214.004). Cleanup liens can also be foreclosed by suit (§ 342.007). Homesteads get much more protection: the Texas Constitution shields a homestead from forced sale for most debts, and Chapter 214 says its lien doesn't apply to a constitutionally protected homestead. A lien can still sit on the title, though, and it has to be dealt with when you sell. Ask a Texas real estate attorney how this applies to your property.

Who enforces codes in the biggest Texas cities

  • Houston: the Department of Neighborhoods, which handles neighborhood inspections and code enforcement.
  • Dallas: the City of Dallas Code Compliance Department, with complaints taken through 311.
  • Fort Worth: the Code Compliance Department, whose Code Enforcement division covers substandard structures, open and vacant buildings, high grass and junk vehicles.
  • San Antonio: the Code Enforcement Division of the Development Services Department; the Building Standards Board hears cases.
  • Austin: Austin Code, whose inspectors issue a Notice of Violation; reports go through 3-1-1.

Can you sell a house with open code violations in Texas?

Yes. Open violations don't stop you from selling, but they shape who will buy. A buyer using a regular mortgage often can't, because lenders and inspectors balk at serious defects. An as-is buyer can.

Disclosure. Most Texas sellers of a single-family home must give the buyer a seller's disclosure notice (Tex. Prop. Code § 5.008), and it asks whether you're aware of "any notices of violations of deed restrictions or governmental ordinances affecting the condition or use of the Property." Some transfers are exempt, such as sales by an executor administering an estate or by court order. Being upfront is the right move either way, and it protects you. With us, you can simply hand over the notices.

How the violations move to the buyer. When we buy, we take the house as-is with the open case attached and deal with the repairs, the city or the demolition question after closing. We don't ask you to make repairs first. Any recorded code liens, cleanup liens or penalty judgments show up in the title search, and the title company pays them from the sale at closing, or the amount is worked out with the city before closing when that's possible. Either way, it's handled in one place, with numbers you see in writing.

Often code problems travel with other ones: back taxes, a vacant house after a death, or a rental that got away from a landlord. See selling a Texas house with back taxes, selling an inherited house in Texas, or our page for tired landlords.

Your options, in one line each

  • Make the repairs: close the case and stop the penalties, if you can fund the work.
  • Ask the city for more time: some deadlines can be extended with a plan.
  • Appeal: within the deadline, if you think the order is wrong; talk to an attorney.
  • List with an agent: possible, but many buyers can't finance a house with open violations.
  • Sell as-is for cash: the violations and liens are handled at closing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell my house with code violations in Texas?
Yes. Open violations don't stop a sale, but they can rule out buyers who need a regular mortgage. An as-is cash buyer can take the house with the violations attached, and recorded liens are handled at closing. We can usually give you an offer within 24 hours.
Do I have to disclose code violations when selling a house in Texas?
Most sellers of a single-family home must give the buyer the Texas seller's disclosure notice, which asks about notices of violations of governmental ordinances affecting the property. Some transfers are exempt, such as certain estate and court-ordered sales. Disclosing protects you, and with us you can just share the notices you've received.
Can the city foreclose on a code lien in Texas?
On non-homestead property, yes in some cases, such as a demolition lien unpaid for more than 180 days when property taxes are also delinquent. A homestead has strong protection under the Texas Constitution, but the lien can still cloud the title. Talk to a Texas real estate attorney about your property.
What happens if I don't repair a substandard building in Texas?
After a hearing, the city can order the building secured, repaired or demolished, usually within 30 days, and can assess daily civil penalties if you don't comply. If the city does the work itself, its costs become a lien on the property. Selling before those costs pile up protects more of your equity.
Will you buy a house the city has ordered demolished?
Often, yes. We look at the order, the deadline and the property, and tell you honestly whether we can buy it and how fast. Send us the address and a copy of the order and we'll usually get back to you within 24 hours.
Who pays the code liens when you buy my house?
Recorded liens show up in the title search and are paid from the sale price at closing, or worked out with the city before closing when that's possible. You'll see the numbers in writing before you sign anything. There are no fees or commissions on our side.
