"The house is still in Mom's name." "Half the family lives out of state, and one cousin won't return calls." "There's a mortgage on the title that Dad paid off twenty years ago." If you've tried to sell and a title company told you no, you're not alone. Many title problems can be cured, but the work is legal, slow and paperwork-heavy, and most families don't want to manage it.

Why a title problem blocks a normal sale

A buyer's lender and title company need to insure the title. If the search shows a gap in ownership, an unreleased lien, or someone else with a claim, the title insurer won't issue a clean policy until it's cured. No policy usually means no loan, and no loan means no closing. That's why a house can sit for months even when the price is right.

Common Florida title problems

  • The owner died and the house is still in their name. Someone needs legal authority to sell, which in Florida usually means probate in the county where the person lived (Fla. Stat. § 733.101).
  • Heirs property with many owners. When a house passes down without planning, it can end up owned by many relatives as tenants in common, and every owner has to sign.
  • Missing or uncooperative heirs. One heir who can't be found or won't sign can stop the whole sale.
  • Homestead descent issues. If the owner left a spouse and children, the spouse generally gets a life estate and the descendants the remainder, unless the spouse elects a one-half interest within 6 months of the death (§ 732.401). A homestead also can't be left by will to someone else if the owner is survived by a spouse or minor child (§ 732.4015), so the will may not control who owns the house.
  • Old mortgages never released. A lender is supposed to record a release within 60 days after payoff (§ 701.04), but lenders merge or disappear and the release never gets recorded.
  • Judgment liens. A recorded judgment is a lien on Florida real estate for an initial 10 years and can be extended (§ 55.10).
  • IRS liens. A federal tax lien reaches all of a taxpayer's property, including real estate (IRS).
  • Deed errors. A wrong legal description, a misspelled name, a missing signature or witness, or a deed that was never recorded.

The Florida tools that clear title

You don't need to learn these, but it helps to know they exist. A Florida title attorney picks the right one for the facts:

  • Formal probate administration: the court appoints a personal representative who can sell the house (§ 733.613).
  • Summary administration: a shorter process when the estate, not counting exempt property, is $150,000 or less, or the owner died more than 2 years ago (§ 735.201; the limit was $75,000 before July 1, 2026).
  • Determination of homestead: a probate court order confirming the house was protected homestead and who inherited it.
  • Quiet title action: a lawsuit that ends with a judgment removing the cloud from the title (§ 65.061).
  • Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act: when co-owning heirs can't agree, Florida's version calls for a court-set value, gives the other owners a chance to buy out those who want to sell, and prefers an open-market sale through a broker over an auction (Fla. Stat. §§ 64.201-64.214).
  • Lien releases and payoffs: tracking down old lenders, paying or settling judgments, or getting an IRS discharge for the property.

Which one fits depends on the facts. Talk to a Florida real estate attorney, or send us the address and we'll walk you through it, free.

What it costs to leave it alone

  • Taxes keep coming. Property taxes are due every November and turn delinquent April 1 (FL Dept. of Revenue), and unpaid years can lead to a tax certificate and, eventually, a tax deed application.
  • The assessment can jump. After a change of ownership, a homestead's capped assessment generally resets to full value the following January 1 unless an exception applies (§ 193.155).
  • A vacant house still needs insurance, upkeep and code compliance. See selling with code violations.
  • The family tree keeps growing. Heirs move, marry and pass away, and each change can add more people who have to sign.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell a house that's still in my deceased parent's name in Florida?
Yes, but someone needs legal authority to sell. That usually means probate in the county where your parent lived, summary administration if the estate is $150,000 or less or your parent died more than 2 years ago, or a court order determining homestead. We buy these houses and work with title attorneys on the paperwork.
What if one heir won't sign?
Every co-owner has to sign a normal sale. If heirs can't agree, Florida's Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act lets the court set a value, gives the other heirs a chance to buy out those who want to sell, and favors an open-market sale. Talk to a Florida real estate attorney, and send us the details so we can tell you what we can do.
Can I sell a house with an old mortgage that was never released?
Often, yes. Florida requires lenders to record a release within 60 days after payoff, but when that never happened, a title attorney can track down the lender or its successor, or use a court process if needed. We buy houses with this problem and coordinate the release with the title company.
What is a quiet title action in Florida?
It's a lawsuit by someone claiming title to land, filed in the county where the property sits, that ends with a judgment removing a cloud from the title. It's used when a claim or defect can't be cleared any other way. It takes time and an attorney, which is one reason a sale to a buyer who handles the curative work can be simpler.
Can every title problem be fixed?
No. Some can't be cured, and some can't be cured at a cost that makes sense for the house. We'll look at the title search and tell you honestly, usually within 24 hours of getting the details.
