How code enforcement works in Georgia

Georgia doesn't have one statewide code office. Each city, and each county for unincorporated areas, adopts its own housing and property-maintenance codes (Macon-Bibb, for example, enforces the International Property Maintenance Code) and enforces them locally. The usual path looks like this:

  1. Complaint or inspection. A neighbor calls, or an inspector spots a problem: a damaged roof, broken windows, an overgrown yard, junk vehicles, an unsafe structure.
  2. Notice of violation. You get a written notice listing the problems and a deadline to fix them.
  3. Citation or hearing. If the deadline passes, the case can go to a local court or, where a city or county has created one, a code enforcement board hearing.
  4. Fines and liens. Fines can be ordered and recorded against the property.
  5. Unsafe-building orders. For houses found unfit, a court can order repair or demolition.

Code enforcement board fines and liens

Under Georgia's Local Government Code Enforcement Boards Act, a board can fine an owner who misses a compliance deadline. For a violation that threatens other people's health or safety, the fine can reach $1,000 a day. For other violations, the total is capped at $1,000. A certified copy of the fine order can be recorded and becomes a lien on the property. If that lien stays unpaid for three months, the board can ask the local government's attorney to foreclose on it. A recorded lien can last up to 20 years.

Unfit buildings: repair, closing and demolition orders

Georgia's unfit-buildings law lets a city or county officer file an in rem complaint against a property found unfit or hazardous. The court hearing is set 15 to 45 days after the complaint is filed. If repair is reasonable compared with the building's value, the order requires repair. If not, it requires demolition. If the owner doesn't comply, the local government can repair, close or demolish the building itself, and must start within 270 days after the owner's deadline.

The cost becomes a lien that ranks ahead of every other lien on the property except taxes. The tax commissioner collects it using the same tools used for unpaid property taxes, including tax foreclosure, with interest and penalties added at the property-tax rate.

Who handles code enforcement in Georgia's biggest cities

  • Atlanta: code complaints go through ATL311 (3-1-1); the Department of City Planning enforces building and zoning codes, and its Codebusters team takes reports of unpermitted work.
  • Savannah: the City of Savannah's Code Compliance office.
  • Augusta: Augusta-Richmond County's Code Enforcement Division enforces property maintenance codes, minimum housing standards, zoning and nuisance rules, and Environmental Services handles demolitions.
  • Columbus: start with Columbus Consolidated Government's 311 Citizen Services.
  • Macon: Macon-Bibb County Code Enforcement, 700 Poplar Street.
  • Athens: Athens-Clarke County's unified government handles code enforcement countywide.

Outside city limits, the county's code enforcement office usually has the case.

Can you sell a house with open code violations in Georgia?

Yes. An open violation doesn't block a sale. It follows the property, so the new owner becomes responsible for bringing it into compliance. That's why most retail buyers, and their lenders, walk away, and why a cash buyer is often the practical route.

Disclosure. Be upfront about what you know. In an agent-listed sale you'll usually fill out a seller's property disclosure statement, and Georgia law requires real estate brokers to disclose adverse material facts they actually know about the property's condition that a buyer couldn't find with a reasonably diligent inspection. Don't hide or misstate a known violation. Talk to a Georgia real estate attorney about exactly what you must disclose. With us, you simply share the notices you've received, and we account for them in the offer.

Liens at closing. The title search shows any recorded fine orders or demolition-cost liens. The title company pays them from the sale proceeds at closing, or the amount is worked out with the local government before closing. Georgia law lets a code enforcement board reduce a fine, and lets a local government release a demolition lien when an owner commits to a rehab plan. Unrecorded open violations transfer to us as the new owner.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell my house with code violations in Georgia?
Yes. Open violations follow the property to the new owner, and recorded fines or liens are paid from the sale proceeds at closing. Many retail buyers won't take that on, but a cash buyer can. We can usually make an offer within 24 hours.
Can a code enforcement lien lead to foreclosure in Georgia?
Yes. A recorded code enforcement board lien that stays unpaid for three months can be foreclosed, and demolition-cost liens under Georgia's unfit-buildings law are collected like property taxes, which can include tax foreclosure.
How much are code violation fines in Georgia?
It depends on the city or county. Where a code enforcement board handles the case, fines can reach $1,000 a day for violations that threaten health or safety, and $1,000 total for other violations. Court fines follow the local ordinance.
Do I have to disclose code violations when selling in Georgia?
You should never hide or misstate a known problem, and Georgia brokers must disclose adverse material facts they know about. Talk to a Georgia real estate attorney about your exact duties. With us, just share the notices and we'll price them in.
What happens if the city orders my house demolished?
If the owner doesn't repair or demolish by the deadline, the local government can do it and must start within 270 days. The cost becomes a lien ahead of everything but taxes. Selling before that point lets the new owner deal with the order.
Who pays the code liens when I sell?
Recorded liens are paid from your sale proceeds at closing, and our offer already accounts for them. Open, unrecorded violations become the buyer's responsibility. You won't owe us any fees.
