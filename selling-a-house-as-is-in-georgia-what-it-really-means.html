Selling a House As-Is in Georgia: What It Really Means

Plain answers from Georgia home buyers who purchase as-is houses every week.

Published October 22, 2026 · MarKey Home Buyers

“As-is” is one of the most misunderstood phrases in Georgia real estate. Sellers sometimes read it as “I can sell this house and never discuss its problems,” and buyers sometimes read it as “something must be badly wrong.” Neither is quite right. Here's what the words actually do — and don't do — in a Georgia sale.

What as-is actually means

Selling as-is means you're selling the house in its current condition: you won't make repairs, and you won't give repair credits. The price is the price, leaking water heater and all. It's a statement about negotiation, not a magic shield — and it doesn't change what you are obligated to tell a buyer.

What you still have to disclose

Georgia is one of the few states with no statutorily required seller disclosure form — it's a “buyer beware” state by tradition. But Georgia courts have long held that buyer-beware has limits, and “as-is” doesn't erase them. In practice, three rules survive any as-is clause: you can't actively hide a defect (painting over water stains before showings is the classic example), you can't stay silent about a serious problem you know about that a buyer couldn't reasonably find on inspection, and if a buyer asks you a direct question, you have to answer it honestly. Sellers who treat as-is as permission to conceal are the ones who end up getting sued after closing. There's also one disclosure no contract language can waive: for most homes built before 1978, federal law requires a lead-based paint disclosure, as-is or not.

The good news: honest disclosure and as-is work fine together. “The roof is 25 years old and the crawl space has moisture damage — priced accordingly, sold as-is” is a perfectly clean way to sell a house in Georgia.

What buyers can still do

Listing a house as-is on the open market doesn't take away the buyer's inspection. Georgia purchase contracts commonly include a due diligence period during which the buyer can inspect and walk away — and as-is buyers use it. What usually happens next is the part sellers don't expect: the inspection report comes back, and the buyer either re-opens the price negotiation anyway or terminates. “As-is” on a listing is a statement of intent, not a guarantee the deal survives the inspection.

Financing adds a second trap door. If your buyer is using an FHA or VA loan, the lender's appraisal has its own minimum property standards — and the lender can require certain repairs before it will fund, no matter what your contract says. On a house that needs real work, an as-is listing can quietly filter you down to cash buyers anyway.

Does as-is mean a lower price?

On the open market, usually yes — buyers read “as-is” as a condition warning and price in the repairs plus a cushion for what the inspection might find. Whether that trade is worth it depends on the house and your timeline. If the house mostly needs cosmetics, listing as-is may cost you more than fixing it. If it needs major repairs you can't fund, or you simply need it gone, as-is is often the rational choice — we've laid out the honest math in cash offer vs. listing with an agent.

Every sale differs — contract terms, what you knew and when, and the facts of your situation all matter. This is general information, not legal advice. For disclosure questions on a specific sale, talk to a Georgia real estate attorney.
