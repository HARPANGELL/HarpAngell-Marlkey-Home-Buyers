When the title stands between you and a sale

"The house is still in Mom's name, and nobody knows how to sell it." "Dad died ten years ago and we never did probate." "There are six of us, and my brother won't return calls." "The title company found a lien from 2009 that nobody remembers." If any of that sounds familiar, you're not stuck forever, but you probably can't sell the usual way right now.

Here's why. Almost every buyer, and every mortgage lender, needs a title insurance policy before closing. A title company won't issue one while there's a "cloud" on the title: a gap in who owns the house, a lien nobody paid off, or a deed with a mistake in it. No policy usually means no loan, and no loan means most buyers walk away.

Common title problems in Texas

  • The owner died and the house is still in their name. Nobody has legal authority to sign a deed yet.
  • Heirs' property. The house passed down without probate, and now many relatives each own a piece.
  • Missing or uncooperative heirs. One heir can't be found, lives overseas, or refuses to sign.
  • Old mortgages that were never released. The loan was paid, but the release was never recorded.
  • Judgment liens. A recorded abstract of judgment creates a lien on non-exempt real property for 10 years (Tex. Prop. Code §§ 52.001, 52.006). A homestead is generally exempt, but the lien can still show up and has to be cleared.
  • IRS liens. A federal tax lien attaches to all of a person's property, including the house.
  • Mechanic's liens. A contractor or supplier who wasn't paid for work on the house.
  • Deed errors. A wrong legal description, a missing signature, a misspelled name, or a deed that was never recorded.

Texas tools for clearing title, in plain English

Texas has several ways to cure title. Which one fits depends on the facts. Here's what each is, without the paperwork:

  • Affidavit of heirship: a sworn family history recorded in the county deed records; once it's been on record five years, Texas courts treat it as prima facie evidence of who the heirs are (Tex. Estates Code § 203.001).
  • Small estate affidavit: for an estate without a will worth no more than $75,000, not counting the homestead and exempt property; it can pass title to the homestead only (§§ 205.001, 205.006).
  • Muniment of title: probating a will without appointing an executor, when the estate owes no unpaid debts other than those secured by real estate (§ 257.001). A will generally must be offered for probate within four years of death (§ 256.003).
  • Determination of heirship: a court proceeding that declares who the legal heirs are.
  • Quiet title suit: a lawsuit asking a court to remove a cloud on title, such as a bad deed or an old claim.
  • Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act: Texas Property Code Chapter 23A, in effect since 2017. When related co-owners can't agree and one asks a court to partition the property, the others get a chance to buy out that share, and the court generally prefers an open-market sale over an auction.

Each has its own requirements, costs and timeline, and some need a court. Talk to a Texas real estate or probate attorney about your situation. Not sure which fits? Send us the address and we'll walk you through it, free.

How we buy houses with title problems

We buy houses that are tangled up, and we work with title attorneys and the title company to do the curative work, so the family doesn't have to run it. In practice, that means:

  • We order a title search first, so everyone knows exactly what the problem is.
  • We make a cash offer for the house as it is, with the title issue taken into account.
  • The title attorney prepares what's needed, such as affidavits, releases or a probate filing. The family members who need to sign get clear instructions, and out-of-town heirs can often sign through a notary near them.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell a house that's still in my deceased parent's name in Texas?
Yes, but someone first needs legal authority to sign, which can come from probate, a muniment of title, a small estate affidavit, or recorded heirship documents, depending on the facts. A Texas probate attorney can tell you which path applies. We can make an offer before that's sorted out and work with a title attorney on the rest.
What if one of the heirs won't sign?
Then the house usually can't be sold with clear title until that heir agrees or a court acts, for example through a partition case under Texas's heirs' property law. Sometimes a clear cash number helps a reluctant heir decide. Send us the address and we'll give you an offer the whole family can look at.
Can you buy a house with an old mortgage or lien that was never released?
Often, yes. If the old loan was paid, the title company or a title attorney can usually track down a release; if it wasn't, it gets paid from the sale at closing. We'll look at it in the title search and explain what we find.
Will a title company insure a house with a title problem?
Not until the problem is resolved or the company agrees to insure over it, which is why these houses are hard to sell to buyers who need a loan. Clearing the title is what makes a title policy, and a normal closing, possible. We work with title attorneys and the title company to get there.
Can every title problem be cured?
No. Some problems take a court case, some take months, and a few can't be resolved in a way a title company will accept. We'll tell you honestly what we see as soon as the title search comes back.
Who pays for the title work when you buy?
We'll spell out in our written offer how curative and closing costs are handled before you sign anything. We charge no fees or commissions, and we pay typical closing costs. Liens that have to be paid come from the sale price at closing.
