How to Sell a House With Tenants in Georgia

Straight answers from Georgia home buyers who purchase occupied rentals every month.

Published October 19, 2026 · MarKey Home Buyers

Short answer: yes, you can sell a tenant-occupied house in Georgia — the tenants don't have to leave first, and in many cases they don't have to leave at all. What you can't do is pretend the lease isn't there. Here's how it actually works.

The lease comes with the house

In Georgia, selling a rental doesn't cancel the lease. If your tenant has a fixed-term lease — say, eight months left on a twelve-month term — the buyer steps into your shoes as landlord and must honor that lease until it ends. The rent, the deposit obligations, and the move-out date all carry over. That's why “I'm selling the house” is not, by itself, a legal reason to make a tenant leave early.

If the tenancy is month-to-month, Georgia law gives you more room: the landlord can end the tenancy with 60 days' written notice (the tenant only needs to give 30). That 60-day clock matters for planning — if you want to deliver the house empty, the notice has to go out well before your target closing date.

Your three real options

1. Sell with the tenants in place. Investors buy occupied rentals all the time — a paying tenant is a feature, not a bug, for a buyer who wants the income. The sale closes, the deposit and lease transfer to the new owner, and the tenant's day-to-day doesn't change except where the rent check goes. This is usually the fastest, lowest-friction path, and it's the one we use most: MarKey buys Georgia rentals with tenants in place, including tenants who are behind on rent or difficult to deal with.

2. Wait out (or end) the tenancy, then sell empty. A vacant house opens the sale up to owner-occupant buyers, who sometimes pay more. The cost is time: the rest of the lease term or the 60-day notice period, plus turnover work, plus the mortgage, taxes, and insurance you carry on an empty house while it sells.

3. Negotiate an early move-out. Often called “cash for keys” — you offer the tenant money or a flexible timeline in exchange for signing an agreement to leave by a set date. It's voluntary on both sides, it's usually cheaper than months of carrying costs, and it keeps everything cooperative.

What not to do

Georgia requires a court dispossessory process to remove a tenant (O.C.G.A. §44-7-50 and following). Changing the locks or removing a tenant's belongings to hurry a sale along can make you liable for trespass, and cutting off utilities to force a tenant out is a misdemeanor (§44-7-14.1) — and no legitimate buyer will ask you to do any of it. Showings are the softer version of the same rule: Georgia doesn't have a statute setting a specific entry-notice period, so your lease controls, and a tenant who feels ambushed by surprise showings can make a listed sale miserable. (One more reason a direct sale — one walkthrough, no sign in the yard — is easier on everyone.)

What this looks like with a cash sale

When we buy an occupied rental, we typically do a single scheduled visit, make a firm offer, and close through a Georgia closing attorney in as little as two weeks. The lease and security deposit transfer at closing, rent gets prorated, and the tenant simply has a new landlord. You stop being one — which, if you've read this far, may be the whole point. If you're weighing this against listing, the honest math is in our breakdown of a cash offer vs. listing with an agent, and the bigger picture for burned-out landlords is in our tired landlord guide.

Every lease and tenancy differs — the lease's own terms, local court practice, and the facts of your situation all matter. This is general information, not legal advice.
