How Long Does Foreclosure Take in Georgia?

Georgia forecloses out of court, so the clock moves fast once it starts. Here's every step, the law behind it, and where your options fit.

Published August 15, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026 · MarKey Home Buyers

Short answer: faster than in most states. Federal rules generally keep a servicer from starting foreclosure until a home loan is more than 120 days behind, but once a Georgia lender starts, the sale can happen roughly five weeks later. That's because Georgia forecloses out of court, under the power of sale in the security deed, with no lawsuit and no judge reviewing the sale first.

The Georgia foreclosure clock, step by step

WhenWhat happensThe rule
Days 1–120Missed payments, late fees, servicer calls and letters. For most home loans the servicer generally can’t make the first foreclosure notice or filing until you’re more than 120 days delinquent.12 CFR §1024.41(f)
At least 30 days before the saleWritten notice of the sale is sent to you by certified or registered mail or overnight delivery. It must name the person with full authority to negotiate, amend or modify your loan.O.C.G.A. §44-14-162.2
The 4 weeks before the saleThe sale is advertised once a week for four weeks in the county’s legal newspaper (the “legal organ”).O.C.G.A. §44-14-162; §9-13-140
Sale dayThe first Tuesday of the month, between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., at the county courthouse. If that Tuesday is New Year’s Day or July 4, the sale moves to Wednesday.O.C.G.A. §9-13-161
After the saleNo general right to buy the house back. If the sale didn’t cover the debt, the lender must get the sale confirmed by a judge within 30 days before it can pursue you for the rest.O.C.G.A. §44-14-161

So how long do you actually have?

Put the rules together and the earliest a Georgia foreclosure sale usually happens is around five months after the first missed payment: the 120-day federal waiting period, then about a month of notices and ads before a first-Tuesday sale. In real life it often takes longer — servicers can be slow to start, and sales get postponed — but you shouldn’t count on that. Once the notice of sale arrives, you’re counting in weeks, not months.

The timing also depends on the calendar. Because sales only happen on first Tuesdays, a lender that starts its notices early in a month can often sell the following month; one that starts late may have to wait a month longer.

What the 120-day window is for

Those first four months are the best time to act, because every option is still open. Federal servicing rules also give you a specific protection: if you send your servicer a complete loss-mitigation application (for a modification, repayment plan or similar) more than 37 days before a scheduled sale, the servicer generally can’t go ahead with the sale until it has evaluated the application and any appeal (12 CFR §1024.41(g)). The key word is complete — ask the servicer for a written list of what they need, and send it all.

Your options at each stage

  • Before the notice of sale: catch up, set up a repayment plan or forbearance, apply for a modification, or talk to a free HUD-approved housing counselor (800-569-4287).
  • After the notice: call the person named in your notice, ask for a written reinstatement figure, and ask whether a complete application would postpone the sale.
  • If the numbers don’t work: selling before the sale date pays the loan off from the proceeds, and whatever equity is left after liens and costs is yours. A financed buyer often needs 30–45 days or more to close, so with a sale date close, a direct cash sale is sometimes the only kind of sale that can fit.
  • If something seems wrong with the notices: talk to an attorney before the sale date, not after.

Nobody can promise in advance that a sale will close in time — title work and the lender’s payoff both take time. If you’re weighing a sale, the earlier you start, the more realistic it is. Our Atlanta foreclosure guide shows where Fulton and DeKalb sales happen and how to look up your ad.

Every situation differs — your loan type, servicer and security deed all matter, and sales are sometimes postponed. This is general information, not legal advice.
