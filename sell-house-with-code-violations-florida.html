A notice on the door. A hearing date. Then a letter saying a fine is running every day until the problem is fixed, and you don't have the money or the time to fix it. That's how a lot of Florida houses end up with code liens bigger than anyone expected.

How code enforcement works in Florida

Florida cities and counties enforce local codes under Chapter 162 of the Florida Statutes, through a code enforcement board or a special magistrate. The usual path:

  1. Complaint. Inspectors can't open a case from an anonymous complaint; the person reporting must give a name and address, unless the inspector believes there's an imminent threat to health or safety (Fla. Stat. § 162.06).
  2. Inspection and notice. The inspector notifies you and gives you a reasonable time to correct the violation. Repeat violations get no correction period, and serious threats can go straight to the board.
  3. Hearing. If it isn't fixed, the board or special magistrate holds a hearing and can order compliance by a deadline.
  4. Fines. Daily fines run until the property complies.
  5. Lien. A certified copy of the fine order recorded in the public records becomes a lien on the land (Fla. Stat. § 162.09).

The board weighs how serious the violation is, what you did to fix it and any past violations, and it can reduce a fine (§ 162.09).

Can a code lien lead to foreclosure in Florida?

Yes, with one big exception. If a lien stays unpaid for 3 months after it's filed, the board can authorize the local government's attorney to foreclose it or sue for a money judgment plus interest. But a code lien can't be foreclosed on homestead property (§ 162.09). That doesn't make it go away: it stays on the title, and a money judgment is still possible. In the City of Miami, a lien "can only be removed by complying the violation and payment of the lien," and the City Attorney may pursue foreclosure, a money judgment or an injunction (City of Miami Code Compliance).

Unsafe structure and demolition orders

When a violation is a serious threat, the local government can make the repairs itself and charge you for them (§ 162.09). Unsafe-structure cases move on their own track:

  • Jacksonville: nobody, including the owner, may enter a condemned or unsafe structure without an active access pass, and the city records demolition and nuisance liens (Jacksonville MCCD).
  • City of Miami: if the Building Official finds a structure more than 50% damaged or beyond repair, it must be demolished; unresolved cases go to the Unsafe Structures panel, and lien charges must be paid before the case closes (City of Miami).
  • Unincorporated Miami-Dade: the County's Consumer and Neighborhood Protection Division handles unsafe structures under Chapter 8 of the County Code, with appeals to the Unsafe Structures Appeal Panel (Miami-Dade County).

Can you sell a house with open code violations in Florida?

Yes. You don't have to fix the house first, but you do have to be honest about it:

  • Pending code case: if you sell after the case starts and before the hearing, you must tell the buyer in writing, give them copies of the notices and pleadings, disclose that they'll be responsible for compliance, and file notice of the sale with the code official within 5 days after the transfer. Skipping the disclosures creates a presumption of fraud (§ 162.06).
  • Known defects: under Johnson v. Davis, a Florida home seller must disclose known facts that materially affect value and aren't readily observable, and an "as is" contract doesn't erase that duty (Florida Bar Journal).
  • Flooding: sellers of residential property must give the buyer a flood disclosure at or before signing the contract (Fla. Stat. § 689.302).

If you're unsure what to disclose, talk to a Florida real estate attorney.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell my house with code violations in Florida?
Yes. You can sell without fixing the violations, as long as you make the required disclosures, including Florida's written disclosure if a code case is pending. Recorded code liens are paid from the sale at closing. We buy houses with open violations and can usually make an offer within 24 hours.
Can the city foreclose on a code lien in Florida?
If a code lien stays unpaid for 3 months, the code board can authorize the local government's attorney to foreclose or sue for a money judgment. A code lien can't be foreclosed on homestead property, but it still stays on the title and a money judgment is possible. Either way, it has to be dealt with to sell.
How much are code enforcement fines in Florida?
State law caps fines at $250 a day for a first violation and $500 a day for a repeat violation. Cities and counties with 50,000 or more people can adopt higher limits, up to $1,000 and $5,000 a day, and irreparable violations can draw up to $15,000. Because fines run daily, the total can grow quickly.
Do I have to disclose code violations when selling a house in Florida?
If a code case is pending, Florida law requires written disclosure to the buyer, copies of the notices, and notice to the code official within 5 days after the transfer. Florida sellers also have to disclose known defects that materially affect value. Talk to a Florida real estate attorney if you're unsure.
Can code enforcement fines be reduced in Florida?
A code board can reduce a fine, and some cities have a process to request a reduction or mitigation after the property complies. Whether that's worth pursuing depends on the house and the amount. Send us the notice and we'll tell you how it affects our offer.
Who pays the code lien when I sell?
Recorded code liens are paid from the sale proceeds at closing, like other liens, unless the amount is reduced before closing. With us, the open violations and repairs become ours after closing. We can usually tell you within 24 hours what we'd offer with the liens attached.
