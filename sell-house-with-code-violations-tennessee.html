How code enforcement works in Tennessee

A notice taped to the door. A hearing date you can't make. Fines that keep adding up every day the house isn't fixed, and repairs you can't afford. If that's where you are, you can still sell the house, as it is.

Most cases start the same way: someone reports the property, an inspector visits, and if there's a violation you get a notice with a deadline. If the work isn't done, the case moves to a hearing, and costs start to pile up. Tennessee gives cities several tools:

  • Unfit or dangerous buildings. A city "public officer" serves a complaint and holds a hearing 10 to 30 days later. If the house is unfit, the order is to repair it, or to vacate and close it, or, if repairs would cost too much compared with its value, to tear it down. If you don't, the city can do the work itself (§ 13-21-103).
  • Overgrown lots and junk. You get 10 days after notice to clean up. If you don't, the city does it and bills you. For an owner-occupied home, a lien attaches only once the charges reach $500 (§ 6-54-113).
  • Citations and fines. Cities can set up administrative hearing officers who hear code citations and levy fines (Title 6, Ch. 54, Part 10), and the biggest cities use environmental courts.

Can a code lien cost you the house?

It can. Repair, demolition and cleanup costs become a lien on the property that ranks right behind tax liens. They're collected by the city tax collector or county trustee the same way as property taxes, and unpaid amounts carry the same penalties and interest as delinquent property taxes (§§ 13-21-103, 6-54-113). That means an unpaid code lien can travel the same road as back taxes, toward a tax suit and a sale. See selling a Tennessee house with tax liens for how that works.

Code enforcement in Tennessee's biggest cities

  • Nashville: Metro Codes and Building Safety, Property Standards Division. Inspectors usually visit within 1 to 5 business days of a complaint and issue an abatement notice. Unresolved cases go to Environmental Court (Wednesdays, 1 p.m., Justice A. A. Birch Building), which can fine up to $50 a day for each violation, plus court costs. Metro Codes says it can't waive court costs or fines or release liens the court assesses.
  • Memphis: City of Memphis Code Enforcement takes complaints through 311. Numerous violations bring a Notice of Hearing. Dangerous buildings can be condemned after a hearing; once condemned, the owner has 10 calendar days to demolish before the City does, and the demolition cost is assessed against the property's taxes. Cases that aren't resolved go to General Sessions Environmental Court.
  • Knoxville: Neighborhood Codes Enforcement, in the Public Service Department, refers unfit or dangerous structures to the Public Officer, who can order repair, boarding up, vacating or demolition. Appeals go to the Better Building Board within 10 days, then to Knox County Chancery Court. If the owner doesn't comply, the City can do the work and place a lien.
  • Chattanooga: City Code Enforcement responds to complaints through 311 and handles minimum housing cases, condemnations, city demolitions, litter and overgrowth (grass has to stay under 10 inches).

Can you sell a house with open code violations in Tennessee?

Yes. Nothing in Tennessee law stops you from selling a house with open violations. What changes is who you sell to, and what you tell them.

What you have to disclose

Tennessee's residential disclosure law covers most sales of homes with one to four units (§ 66-5-201). The seller gives the buyer either a disclosure statement about the property's condition, including known material defects, or, if the buyer waives that, a disclaimer saying the buyer takes the house as-is (§ 66-5-202). Some sales are exempt, including when the owner hasn't lived in the house at any point in the three years before the sale, and sales by an estate's personal representative (§ 66-5-209). A buyer who gets a disclosure and later finds an undisclosed defect can sue for actual damages within one year (§ 66-5-208). Whatever form you use, tell your buyer in writing about any notices, citations and liens you know about. Talk to a Tennessee real estate attorney if you're unsure.

How the violations and liens get handled

  • Open violations come with the house. When we buy it, we become the owner who answers to the city after closing.
  • Recorded liens and assessed costs show up in the title search and are paid from the sale price at closing, so the buyer gets clear title.
  • Reductions are sometimes possible; Memphis, for example, has a lien waiver hearing process. Whether a city or court will reduce anything is up to them.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell my house with code violations in Tennessee?
Yes. You can sell with open violations, fines or even a condemnation order. A cash buyer can take the house as-is, and recorded liens are paid from the sale at closing. We can usually make you an offer within 24 hours.
Do I have to disclose code violations when selling a house in Tennessee?
Tennessee requires either a disclosure statement or, if the buyer waives it, an as-is disclaimer for most sales of one- to four-unit homes, with some exemptions. Either way, tell the buyer in writing about notices, citations and liens you know about, and ask a Tennessee real estate attorney if you're unsure.
How much are Environmental Court fines in Nashville?
Nashville's Environmental Court can impose fines of up to $50 a day for each violation, plus court costs, and Metro Codes says it can't waive those fines or release liens the court assesses. The longer a case stays open, the more it can cost.
Can a code lien lead to losing my house in Tennessee?
It can. Tennessee lets cities collect repair, cleanup and demolition costs the same way as property taxes, with the same penalties and interest, so an unpaid code lien can follow the same path as delinquent taxes toward a tax suit and sale. Talk to a Tennessee real estate attorney about your case.
Will you buy a condemned house in Memphis or Knoxville?
Yes, we look at condemned houses across Tennessee. Time matters: in Memphis, once a building is condemned, the owner has 10 calendar days to demolish it before the City does. Send us the address and the order and we'll tell you quickly what we can do.
