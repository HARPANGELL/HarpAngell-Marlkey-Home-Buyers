When a house feels "locked" after a death

Losing a parent or a spouse is hard enough. Then comes the house: still full of their things, still in their name, still collecting tax bills and maybe a mortgage payment. Maybe your siblings live in three different states. Maybe nobody knows whether there was a will. It's common to feel like the house is locked and nobody has the key.

The key, legally, is authority to sign a deed. When someone dies in Texas, their house doesn't transfer in a way a title company will accept until there's a court order or recorded documents showing who now owns it, or who can sell it. Until then, a buyer can't get title insurance, so a normal sale can't close.

Texas probate basics, in plain English

Which path fits depends mostly on whether there's a will, what the estate owes, and whether the family agrees. One line each:

  • Independent administration: an executor or administrator handles the estate with little court supervision, if the will allows it or all the heirs agree (Tex. Estates Code ch. 401).
  • Muniment of title: the will is probated as proof of who owns the property, with no executor, when the estate has no unpaid debts other than those secured by real estate (ch. 257).
  • Four-year deadline: a will generally must be offered for probate within four years of death, with narrow exceptions (§ 256.003).
  • No will: heirs are set by Texas law and can be documented through an affidavit of heirship, a court determination of heirship, or, for a small estate, a small estate affidavit that can pass the homestead (chs. 202, 203, 205).

In the big metros, these cases are heard in the county probate courts for Harris, Dallas, Tarrant, Bexar and Travis counties. When heirs disagree or live far away

Inherited houses often have several owners at once, and they don't always agree. One sibling wants to keep it, one wants to rent it, and one just wants it gone. When related co-owners can't agree and someone asks a court to divide the property, Texas's Uniform Partition of Heirs' Property Act (Tex. Prop. Code ch. 23A) gives the others a chance to buy out that share, and it favors an open-market sale over an auction. That protects families, but it means court, time and legal fees.

Often the simpler path is a single, written cash number everyone can look at. Heirs who live outside Texas can usually review the offer by email and sign closing documents with a notary near them, through the title company. Nobody has to fly in to clean out the house.

The bills keep coming while you decide

  • Property taxes. A new tax lien attaches every January 1, and unpaid taxes are delinquent after January 31, with penalties and interest from February 1 (Tex. Tax Code §§ 32.01, 31.02, 33.01). See selling a Texas house with back taxes.
  • The mortgage. Federal law generally keeps a lender from calling the loan due just because the house passed to a relative when the borrower died (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d)), and servicers have to work with heirs they've confirmed as successors in interest (12 CFR 1024.31). But the payments are still due. If they stop, the lender can foreclose; see facing foreclosure in Texas.
  • Upkeep. Insurance, utilities, lawn care, and the risk of break-ins or code violations at a vacant house.

Every month the house sits, those costs come out of the inheritance.

What happens to the money when you sell

At closing, the title company pays off the mortgage, any back taxes or liens, and the closing costs from the sale price. The rest goes to whoever is selling: the estate, through the executor or administrator, or the heirs according to their shares. Everyone sees the same settlement statement, which helps keep family conversations simple. When we make an offer, we look at whose name is on the deed, whether there's a will, what's owed on the house, its condition, and the family's timeline, and we put the numbers in writing.

Your options, in one line each

  • Keep it: move in, or one heir buys out the others.
  • Rent it: income, but also repairs, tenants and landlord duties.
  • List it with an agent: once the title is clear, and usually after repairs and a cleanout.
  • Sell it as-is for cash: no repairs, no cleanout, and the mortgage and taxes are paid at closing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell an inherited house before probate is finished in Texas?
It depends on who has authority to sign. An executor or administrator can often sell during the estate, and in some cases a muniment of title or heirship documents are enough. We can make an offer early and close once the authority is in place.
How long do I have to probate a will in Texas?
Generally, a will must be offered for probate within four years of the person's death; after that, it's allowed only in narrow situations. If you're close to that deadline, talk to a Texas probate attorney soon. We're happy to make an offer while you sort it out.
What if my siblings don't agree about selling the inherited house?
Co-owners who can't agree may end up in a partition case, where Texas's heirs' property law gives family members a chance to buy each other out. That takes time and legal fees. A single written cash offer often helps everyone see the real numbers.
Who pays the mortgage and property taxes on an inherited house?
The bills don't stop when the owner dies. Taxes keep accruing and the mortgage payments are still due, even though federal law generally keeps the lender from calling the loan due just because it passed to a relative. If you sell, both are paid from the sale price at closing.
Do we have to clean out the house before selling?
Not with us. We buy inherited houses as-is, including the furniture and belongings. Take what you want to keep and leave the rest.
Can heirs who live out of state sell a Texas house?
Yes. Out-of-state heirs can usually review the offer remotely and sign with a notary near them through the title company. Send us the address and we'll explain how it would work for your family.
