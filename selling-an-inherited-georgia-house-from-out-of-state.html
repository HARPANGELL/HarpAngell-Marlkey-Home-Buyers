Selling an Inherited Georgia House From Out of State

You live in Ohio, the house is in Georgia, and every week it sits empty costs the estate money. Here's how out-of-state heirs actually get it sold — usually without a single flight.

Published October 19, 2026 · MarKey Home Buyers

Inheriting a house is complicated enough. Inheriting one three states away adds a layer most people don't see coming: who checks on it, who mows the lawn, who meets the contractor, and how do you sign closing papers from 700 miles away? The good news is that out-of-state heirs sell Georgia houses every day, and the process is far more remote-friendly than most people expect.

You almost never have to be there in person

Georgia is an attorney-closing state — a licensed Georgia attorney conducts the closing and handles the funds. That actually works in your favor as a remote seller, because closing attorneys here routinely run “mail-away” closings: they overnight the documents to you, you sign in front of a notary wherever you live, and you send them back before the closing date. Many sellers instead sign a limited power of attorney so someone local — often the attorney's office itself — can execute the paperwork for them. Either way, your sale proceeds arrive by wire. If probate is involved, most of that happens remotely too: filings go through the probate court in the Georgia county where your relative lived, and a Georgia probate attorney can handle the petitions without you appearing for most routine matters. We walk through that process in How Does Probate Work in Georgia?

Know about Georgia's 3% nonresident withholding

Here's the one that surprises out-of-state sellers at the closing table. Under Georgia law (O.C.G.A. § 48-7-128), when a nonresident of Georgia sells Georgia real estate, the buyer is generally required to withhold 3% of the purchase price and send it to the Georgia Department of Revenue. It isn't an extra tax — it's a prepayment toward any Georgia income tax you might owe on the sale, and you claim it back when you file a Georgia nonresident return.

For inherited houses, there's an important relief valve: because your tax basis usually “steps up” to the home's value at the date of death, your actual taxable gain is often small or zero. Georgia lets the seller sign an affidavit of seller's gain (Form IT-AFF2), so the 3% is calculated on the gain instead of the full price — which can shrink the withholding dramatically or eliminate it. The closing attorney prepares this routinely; just raise it early. For the full tax picture, see Taxes on Selling an Inherited House in Georgia.

The real enemy is distance plus time

A vacant house you can't drive past is a liability that compounds. Standard homeowner's policies often restrict or lapse coverage once a home sits empty, so the estate may need pricier vacant-property insurance. Lawns grow, code-violation notices go to an address nobody checks, pipes fail with nobody there to catch it, and empty houses in any Georgia market attract break-ins and squatters. Add property taxes, utilities you keep on to protect the house, and — if there's a loan on it — monthly mortgage payments (see Inherited a House With a Mortgage — What Now?). Every month of long-distance limbo is money leaking out of the inheritance.

Your two realistic paths

List it with a local agent. This can net the highest price if the house is in good shape — but from out of state it usually means paying to clear out a lifetime of belongings, hiring and supervising repairs by phone, and keeping the house show-ready for weeks or months while it sits on the market. You'll also need someone local for every inspection and walkthrough.

Sell it as-is for cash. A direct sale to a local buyer skips the repairs, the clean-out (take what matters to the family, leave the rest), the showings, and most of the coordination. One video walkthrough is typically enough for us to make a firm offer, and we close through a Georgia attorney on your timeline — with mail-away documents so you never have to board a plane. For houses that need work, or families who simply want this chapter closed, the speed and certainty often outweigh the difference in price. And if several heirs are involved, everyone on the deed will need to sign — we cover that in Do All Heirs Have to Agree to Sell an Inherited House in Georgia?

Every estate is different — how title passed, probate status, and your home state's taxes all change the details. This is general information, not legal or tax advice. A Georgia probate or real estate attorney and a tax professional can advise on your specific situation.
