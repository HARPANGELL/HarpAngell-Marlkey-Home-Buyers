Do All Heirs Have to Agree to Sell an Inherited House in Georgia?

Straight answers from Georgia home buyers who deal with estate properties every week.

Sometimes yes, sometimes no — and the difference comes down to one question: who holds title to the house right now? If the estate still holds it, the personal representative may be able to sell without a family vote. If title has already passed to the heirs, every co-owner has to sign the deed. Here's how to tell which situation you're in.

When the executor can sell without unanimous agreement

If there's a will and it grants the executor a power of sale — most attorney-drafted Georgia wills do — the executor can generally sell estate property without getting every beneficiary's signature. The heirs receive their share of the proceeds through the estate instead of signing the deed themselves. Beneficiaries who believe the executor is selling below fair value or otherwise mishandling the estate can raise that with the probate court, but they can't simply veto the sale.

If there's no will, or the will doesn't clearly grant a power of sale, the administrator or executor usually needs leave to sell from the probate court before conveying real estate. Heirs get formal notice of that petition and a chance to object — so in practice, an administrator's sale gives disagreeing heirs a real forum, even though the final word belongs to the judge rather than to any one holdout.

When every heir has to sign

Once title has passed out of the estate — through a deed of assent, through an order that no administration is necessary, or because the estate was never opened and the heirs simply inherited as a group — the heirs own the house together as tenants in common. At that point there's no executor to act for anyone. Selling the whole house requires every co-owner (and in some cases their spouses) to sign, no matter how small someone's share is. A 5% owner can stall a sale just as effectively as a 50% owner.

This is also the rule for Georgia's "No Administration Necessary" shortcut: it only works when all heirs unanimously agree in a signed, notarized agreement. One holdout means it's off the table.

What if one heir refuses to sell?

You're not stuck forever. The usual paths, roughly in order of friendliness:

Buy them out. The willing heirs purchase the holdout's fractional share (or the other way around) at a price tied to a real appraisal. This is almost always cheaper than court.

Sell your own share. A co-owner can sell their fractional interest without anyone's permission — though fractional interests sell at a steep discount, so this is rarely the best outcome.

File a partition action. Any co-owner can ask the superior court to divide or sell the property. Because most inherited homes can't be physically split, partition usually ends in a court-ordered sale with proceeds divided by ownership share. Georgia adopted the Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act in 2012 (O.C.G.A. § 44-6-180 and following), and it gives families real protections: the court gets the property appraised, the heirs who didn't ask for the sale get roughly 45 days to elect to buy out the ones who did at the appraised value, and if a sale does happen the court favors an open-market sale over an auction on the courthouse steps. That's designed to stop outside investors — or one impatient heir — from forcing a fire-sale price on everybody else.

The practical way through

Most families never need a judge. What they need is a number everyone can look at. A written cash offer on the house as-is gives every heir the same concrete figure — each person can see exactly what their share is worth today, with no repairs, no agent commissions, and no months of carrying costs while the family argues. We regularly make offers on estate houses precisely so families have that baseline, even if they ultimately choose to list instead.

Every family's situation differs — who signed what, whether an estate was opened, and how the will reads all change the answer. This is general information, not legal advice. Talk to a Georgia probate attorney about your specific situation.
