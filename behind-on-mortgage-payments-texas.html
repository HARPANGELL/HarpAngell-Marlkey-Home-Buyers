Behind on Mortgage Payments in Texas? Here's What Happens Next

Missing a payment or two doesn't mean you've lost the house. Here's the Texas timeline and the options you still have.

Quick answerIf you're behind on a Texas mortgage, federal rules require your servicer to try to reach you by the 36th day of delinquency and send written options by the 45th day, and generally bar the first foreclosure notice or filing until you're more than 120 days behind. After that, Texas requires only a notice of default with at least 20 days to cure and a 21-day notice of sale before a first-Tuesday trustee sale. The earlier you act, the more options you have, including a repayment plan, a loan modification, or selling the house.

What happens as the missed payments add up

Maybe it was a layoff, a medical bill, a divorce, or a payment that jumped when the escrow went up. However it started, the late notices are stacking up, and you're wondering how long you have before the house is at risk. Here's the honest answer for Texas.

How far behindWhat usually happens
About 30 daysA late fee is charged under your mortgage note once its grace period ends (check your note for the amount). By the 36th day of delinquency, your servicer must try to reach you live and tell you about options (12 CFR 1024.39(a)).
About 60 daysBy the 45th day, the servicer must send a written notice with a contact person, examples of loss mitigation options, and how to find a HUD-approved housing counselor (12 CFR 1024.39(b)). Late payments can be reported to the credit bureaus.
About 90 daysMore late fees, and often a formal default or "breach" letter warning that the loan can be accelerated. In Texas, a notice of default on a home you live in must give you at least 20 days to cure (Tex. Prop. Code § 51.002(d)).
More than 120 daysThe point at which federal rules generally allow the first foreclosure notice or filing (12 CFR 1024.41(f)). In Texas, that means a notice of sale at least 21 days before a first-Tuesday trustee sale (§ 51.002(b)).

Servicers differ on exactly when letters go out, so treat the middle rows as typical, not fixed. The federal deadlines and the Texas notice periods are the parts set by law.

Why Texas is different: the end can come quickly

In many states, foreclosure means a court case that takes months or years. Most Texas loans are foreclosed without going to court, through a trustee sale on the first Tuesday of the month. Once the servicer can start, the 20-day cure period and the 21-day notice of sale can put a sale date about six weeks out. Home equity loans are the exception: the Texas Constitution requires a court order to foreclose them.

After a typical mortgage trustee sale, Texas gives you no general right to buy the house back. If the sale brings less than you owe, the lender can sue for the difference within two years (§ 51.003). That's why the months before a notice of sale are the most valuable time you have. For the full sale-by-sale picture, see facing foreclosure in Texas.

When selling makes sense

Selling isn't the right answer for everyone. It tends to make sense when:

  • You could catch up once, but the payment still won't fit your budget going forward.
  • The house needs repairs you can't pay for, so listing it isn't realistic.
  • You have equity and don't want to risk losing it at a trustee sale.
  • There are other problems stacked on the mortgage, such as back taxes, liens or code violations.
  • The house was inherited or is sitting vacant, and nobody wants to keep paying on it. Our page on selling an inherited house in Texas covers that.

Here's how a sale handles the loan: the title company orders a payoff statement from your servicer, and at closing the loan balance, the missed payments, late fees and any other liens are paid from the sale price. The lender releases its lien, and whatever's left is yours. If you're already past the point of a notice of sale, a sale that closes before the trustee sale pays off the loan, so the foreclosure sale doesn't happen, as long as there's time to close.

Frequently asked questions

How many mortgage payments can you miss before foreclosure in Texas?
Federal rules generally keep a servicer from making the first foreclosure notice or filing until you're more than 120 days behind, which is roughly four missed payments. After that, Texas requires a notice of default with at least 20 days to cure and a notice of sale at least 21 days before the sale. If you're already behind, we can show you your numbers within 24 hours.
Will my lender work with me if I'm behind on my mortgage?
Many servicers offer repayment plans, forbearance or loan modifications, and federal rules require them to contact you early in a delinquency and explain options. Call your servicer and ask, and consider a free HUD-approved housing counselor. A cash offer from us can sit alongside those options while you decide.
Can I sell my house if I'm behind on payments in Texas?
Yes. You can sell at any point before a foreclosure sale. The title company pays the loan, the missed payments and fees from the sale price at closing, and you keep what's left. Send us the address and we'll make an offer, usually within 24 hours.
What if I owe more than my house is worth?
Then a regular sale may not cover the loan, and options like a loan modification, a short sale or a deed in lieu may fit better; each needs your lender's approval. We'll tell you honestly if our offer can't cover the payoff. A Texas real estate attorney or a HUD-approved counselor can help you weigh the rest.
How fast can you buy my house in Texas?
We can close in as little as 14 days once title is clear, or on whatever date works for you. Most of that time is the title company confirming the payoff and the title. Send us the address and we'll give you a realistic date with our offer.
