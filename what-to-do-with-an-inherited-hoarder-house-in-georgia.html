What to Do With an Inherited Hoarder House

You opened the door, and the house you inherited is packed to the ceiling. Here's a calm, practical order of operations — and why you may not have to clean out anything at all.

Published October 19, 2026 · MarKey Home Buyers

Few inheritances are harder to face than a hoarder house. Decades of belongings stacked in every room, paths instead of floors, and a family already grieving now staring down what looks like months of work. If this is where you are, the two most useful things to know are these: you don't have to fix it all, and you don't have to decide anything this week. Houses like this sell in Georgia all the time — often exactly as they stand.

First: secure it, and don't rent a dumpster yet

The instinct is to attack the piles immediately. Resist it for a little while. Hoarder houses routinely have real money buried in them — cash in coat pockets and coffee cans, jewelry, savings bonds, coin collections — and documents the estate will actually need: the deed, insurance policies, bank and mortgage statements, tax records, maybe the will itself. A hasty cleanout can throw away exactly the paperwork that makes the rest of the process easier. Change the locks, make sure the homeowner's insurance stays active (vacant houses are a particular problem for coverage), and do a slow, careful sweep for valuables and papers before anything goes in a bag.

Second: make sure you have the authority to act

If the estate is going through probate, the contents of the house belong to the estate — not to whichever relative gets there first with a truck. The executor or administrator is the one with legal authority over what's kept, distributed, or discarded, and clearing out property before that authority is in place can create real friction with other heirs and, in contested families, real legal problems. If probate hasn't started, that's the first move; we explain the process in How Does Probate Work in Georgia? And if several siblings inherited together, everyone on the deed will eventually need to sign a sale — see Do All Heirs Have to Agree to Sell an Inherited House in Georgia?

What a full cleanout really involves

Be honest about the scale before committing to it. A true hoarder cleanout is not a weekend with a rented dumpster — it's typically multiple dumpster loads, weeks of labor or a paid cleanout crew, and sometimes biohazard-level cleaning if there's been animal activity, pest infestation, or mold under the piles. The weight of stored belongings can also hide what it's done to the house: water damage nobody saw happening, buckled flooring, blocked vents. Families who start down this road often discover that after the cleanout comes a second project — repairs — before the house is anywhere near listable. All of it paid for out of pocket or out of the estate, while property taxes, insurance, and utilities keep running.

You can sell it without cleaning it out

This is the part most families don't know. A cash buyer who purchases houses as-is will take a hoarder house with the contents in it. The family walks through, takes the photos, papers, and keepsakes that matter, and leaves everything else — the buyer deals with the cleanout after closing. That turns a months-long, five-figure project into a single decision. Selling as-is is routine and legal in Georgia; there's no law requiring a house to be emptied, repaired, or updated before it sells. Georgia doesn't mandate a standardized seller disclosure form either, but honesty still applies: a seller can't conceal a serious hidden problem they actually know about, and must answer a buyer's direct questions truthfully. For an executor, “I never lived here and I'm selling it exactly as my parent left it” is about as clean as an as-is sale gets — more on that in Selling an Estate House As-Is: What Executors Should Know.

Weighing your two paths

Clean out, repair, and list. If the house is in a strong market and the family has the time, money, and stomach for the project, this can bring the highest gross price. Just run the real math: cleanout costs, repair costs, months of carrying costs, agent commissions, and the emotional toll of sorting a loved one's possessions piece by piece — against what the house actually nets at the end.

Sell as-is for cash. One walkthrough, a firm offer, take what matters and leave the rest, and close on the estate's timeline through a Georgia closing attorney. For houses with serious contents and deferred maintenance, the gap between the two paths is usually far smaller than families expect once every cost is counted — and the fastest way to know is to get a real number. The good news on taxes: thanks to the stepped-up basis, selling an inherited house usually triggers little or no capital gains tax — see Taxes on Selling an Inherited House in Georgia.

Every estate is different — probate status, who holds authority over the estate, liens, and the house's condition all change the picture. This is general information, not legal or tax advice. A Georgia probate attorney can advise on your specific situation.
